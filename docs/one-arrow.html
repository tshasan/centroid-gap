RAG · vector search · proved in Lean 4

One label can’t point two ways

A RAG system stores text chunks as vectors and sorts them into folders. To answer a question it doesn’t read every chunk. It compares the question with one label per folder, then opens the best-matching folders first. When a folder mixes two topics that point in opposite directions, its label can’t point at both, and the search opens the wrong folders first. The proof covers exactly that case: opposite directions in one folder. Topics that are merely different, which is the usual case in real embeddings, are not covered by it.

● chunk of text → folder label (the folder’s average vector) ● your question similar meaning = similar direction
04 · In practice

What this means for real systems

−7%

The boost knob, learned. ivf_tune learns one boost per folder from real questions. On two 1M-vector benchmarks it cut search work by 6.7% and 7.3% at the same recall (6.0% and 8.7% when read at exactly 95% recall; one index build, and a second build gave smaller whole-probe gains), with no index rebuild.

PR ≈38

Real folders are spread out. Real folders are fuzzy clouds, not neat pairs: their spread has a participation ratio of about 38, a measure of how many directions share the variance. In our tests extra labels per folder cost more than they saved.

1 → many

Multi-topic chunks. A chunk that covers two topics gets one vector, an average that may match neither. Multi-vector retrieval (several vectors per document) is built to avoid this. Our proof is about a folder’s routing label, not a chunk’s embedding, and we haven’t measured either on RAG chunks.

The 2-D picture is the exact planar construction from the paper: k folders, each holding two opposite chunks; questions point at single chunks. Any one-label router (label + boost per folder) opens at least (k+3)/4 folders on average, and that bound is reached. Two labels per folder open 1.
