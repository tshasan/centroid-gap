Centroids pick the wrong lists

Vector search engines split their data into lists and choose which lists to open by each list's average point. This project proves that this choice can waste most of the search work, even when the lists are the best k-means can find, and measures how much is wasted on real embeddings.

What this means

  1. How the search works

    An inverted-file (IVF) index, as in Faiss, stores vectors in lists and keeps one centroid per list, the list's mean. A query ranks the lists by distance to their centroids and scans the closest few. Each list opened is a probe; every vector scanned costs one distance.

  2. Where it goes wrong

    A mean describes the middle of a list, not its edges. When a query's true neighbour sits near the edge of its list, another list's centroid can look closer, and the search pays to scan lists that do not hold the answer.

  3. What the proofs show

    This is not a quirk of bad clustering. Even the unique globally optimal k-means index, with exactly equal list sizes, can rank the answer's list last. The extra work grows like √N, which is the largest order possible. Changing only the representatives, with the lists untouched, removes the gap.

  4. What the experiments show

    On two million-vector benchmarks an oracle told where the answers are did 70% and 83% less work. That is an upper bound on what any router could save. Retrained routing vectors lost to the centroids; one learned bias per list (ivf_tune) saved 6–9%, about a tenth of the oracle gap.

Why it matters

Standard IVF indexes, such as Faiss's, route by centroid, and it is natural to assume that lower k-means loss means cheaper search. The proofs show that assumption can fail by a factor of order √N. The benchmarks show that at high recall, most of the scanning goes to lists that hold none of the answers.

Whether the remaining savings are easier to find in the router or in the partition is open. We tested routers on fixed lists; a partition trained on query neighbours is still untested.
