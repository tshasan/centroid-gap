CG-1 · Centroid gap · vector search unit

Search by the average, miss the answer.

Vector databases split millions of vectors into lists. To answer a query they score only each list's average point, its centroid, and open the few lists that look closest. But a query's nearest neighbor often sits at the edge of its list, far from the average. This unit shows what that costs, and the one number per list that can fix it.

Route a query

Six lists built by k-means. Each colored region is where its centroid scores best. Drag the orange query. The index opens lists in centroid order until it reaches the list that holds the true nearest neighbor. Every opened list costs a scan of all its points. Turn a knob to add a bias to that list's score, or press Tune to fit all six from sample queries.

02

The trap, and the silver bullet

A construction from the paper. Each blue row has one raised point, the tip, which is the true answer. Its grey decoy row sits higher on average. The queries come from very far above, so only height matters, and the highest centroid opens first. Every decoy beats the true list, even though these lists are the unique best k-means clustering. Then press the orange button.

No two balanced indexes with the same number of lists can differ by more than the ceiling shown below. This planar trap reaches about half of it at large N. On the unit sphere the paper proves an optimal k-means index that reaches the ceiling to within 1/(4√N).

03

On real data

One million Yandex Text-to-Image vectors, 1,000 lists, work needed to find 95% of each query's 10 nearest neighbors. The oracle knows which lists hold the answers. It is not a real router, but it shows how much is left on the table.

On real data there is no silver bullet. Scoring the same lists better wins back little of the gap.

Centroids: 34 lists, 34,529 distance evaluations per query. Oracle: 6 lists, 5,774. One bias per list (ivf_tune): −8.7% [95% CI −10.9, −6.5], index seed 2026; its default settings were not chosen on held-out data. Paper, Tables 1 and 2.
